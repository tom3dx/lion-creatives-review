Film & Video · Brand & Digital · Technology

The film and the product were made in the same room.

A regional fintech had a small-business lending product with a genuinely different way of deciding, a launch date fixed by a partner bank, and two agencies building toward each other in the dark.

Client
A regional fintech — anonymised at their request
Sector

Constraint

01The challenge

The product was ready before anyone could explain it.

The lending product made a decision no one else in its category made: it read a business's cash-flow behaviour rather than leaning on a credit score alone. That difference was real, defensible, and completely invisible until after you had already applied.

Everything before that moment — the ads, the website, the sales call — used the same seven words as every competitor. Fast approval. Real humans. Built for small business.

Two agencies had already been briefed, separately. One was scripting a campaign around a promise the website could not demonstrate. The other was rebuilding a sitemap the campaign was never going to send traffic to. Both were on schedule. Neither was going to work, and the launch date was tied to a partner bank's calendar, so the honest options were narrow.

02The strategic choice

Stop selling the promise. Show the decision.

The problem, stated plainly

Every claim available in this category is already owned by four competitors. A film that asserts speed, or humanity, or understanding, is indistinguishable from the film the competitor shot last quarter — and no amount of craft rescues an undifferentiated claim. The only thing the client owned outright was the behaviour of the product itself.

What we decided

Make the underwriting logic the creative idea, in both media.

The film would not describe how the decision works. It would follow one owner through a single week — the Monday deposits, the slow Tuesday, the invoice that finally clears on Friday — and end at the instant the platform reads that same week and says yes. The last frame of the film is the decision.

The site would then open on exactly that: an interactive read of a business's cash-flow shape, available before signup, before an account, before a credit pull. The film ends where the product begins. Same idea, carried twice, in the two media that could each only do half the job.

That demanded one structural change, and it was the real decision: a single team on a single backlog. The film's closing beat and the product's first screen are the same composition. Two vendors cannot ship a shared frame against a fixed date — they can only negotiate about it. So the campaign and the build were merged into one engagement, with one creative director, one tech lead, and one weekly review that both sides had to survive.

What we rejected, and why

A hero brand film, with the website rebuilt alongside it
The safe route, already in motion, and the one everybody expected to approve. Rejected because the differentiator lives in the product's behaviour, and a film can only assert behaviour. Assertion is precisely what every competitor has already bought.
A testimonial-led campaign
Persuasive, quick to produce, and cheap. Rejected on honesty before timing: at launch there were no customers who had used this version of the product, and we were not going to stage people describing an experience they had not had.
Shipping the interactive read as a phase two
The most tempting compromise, because it protects the launch date on paper. Rejected because it moves the only piece of genuine proof to a date after the media spend has already run. Phase two is where differentiators go to die.
Keeping both agencies and adding a producer between them
Rejected because coordination is not authorship. A producer can align two calendars; they cannot make two companies agree on a single frame at eleven at night, three days before a date nobody is allowed to move.

The trade we accepted. A smaller site at launch. Whole branches of the old sitemap were not rebuilt, because the one screen that proved the claim needed the engineering time more than the About page did. We said that out loud in the first week, in writing, so that nobody discovered it in the last one.

03The work

One idea, carried across a film, a stills set, and a product surface.

The film was shot over three days in two real businesses, with the owners' own staff on the floor. Nothing was cast to look like a small business; the standing constraint was that every frame had to survive the owner watching it.

The edit is built as a compression. It opens in something close to real time and tightens, day by day, until an entire week fits inside a single breath — which is the same operation the underwriting performs on the data.

The hand-off frame

The last shot of the film is the first screen of the product.

Same crop, same angle, same weight of type. Someone who watches the film and then arrives on the site should not feel that they have left it — they should feel they have started using it.

Photography

Shot on the film set, in the same hour, on the same two lenses.

The stills were not a second shoot with a second look. Pulling them out of the film days meant the campaign imagery, the site headers and the sales deck all sit in one grade — and the client's team stopped having to ask which photograph belongs to which channel.

The edit locked on a Thursday. The screen it hands off to shipped the following Tuesday. The same person approved both.

04Craft decisions

Small decisions, defended.

None of these appeared in a brief and none of them would be missed individually. Collectively they are the difference between work that holds together and work that merely matches.

  1. 01

    Two lenses for the entire film

    A 35 and an 85, nothing else on the truck. The grade holds across three days and two locations because no shot was ever made wide enough to argue with the rest of them.

  2. 02

    No score under the decision beat

    Music drops out entirely for the final passage and the film finishes on room tone. Scoring that moment would have told the audience how to feel about a claim we wanted them to simply watch happen.

  3. 03

    One angle, sampled once

    The diagonal in the film's title cards, the crop on the campaign stills and the section cuts in the interface are the same angle, taken from the identity's own geometry. Sampled once as a token and reused everywhere — never eyeballed per surface.

  4. 04

    Interface motion timed against the edit

    Transition durations were pulled from the film's cut rhythm rather than a framework default, and every easing curve in the build is the same curve. Motion that inherits from the edit reads as one authorship; motion that inherits from a library reads as a template.

  5. 05

    Tabular figures in the money column

    Every number in the cash-flow read is set with tabular numerals, so a value updating in place does not shift the digits beside it. In a lending product, type that twitches while someone reads a balance is not a cosmetic problem.

  6. 06

    The proof screen renders without JavaScript

    Server-rendered first, enhanced afterwards. The single screen carrying the whole argument was not allowed to depend on a bundle arriving over a phone connection in a parking lot, which is where a good deal of this audience actually reads things.

  7. 07

    Red does exactly one job

    It marks the decision — the beat in the film, the approval state in the product, the active step in the application. Nowhere else, in either medium. A signal used sparingly is a signal; used everywhere it is decoration.

05Outcome

What changed inside the client's operation.

The sales conversation changed shape. Reps stopped opening with the promise and started opening with the screen. The demo became the pitch, which meant the first two minutes of every call were about the prospect's own business rather than about the lender.

Marketing and product stopped arguing about the homepage. It was no longer a marketing page describing a product; it was a product surface with an owner, a backlog and a release process. The argument that had run for a year simply lost its subject.

The film outlived its campaign window. It is now the first thing a new underwriter watches on their first day, because it turned out to be the clearest explanation of how the decision works that anyone in the company had ever written down.

What we would do differently. We under-scoped the content model. The client's team needed to publish new business examples themselves, and in the first version that was an engineering ticket rather than a form. We rebuilt it after launch at our own cost, because it was our misjudgement and not a change of scope.

We do not publish performance figures. The client's numbers are theirs to disclose, and a percentage without its baseline, its market and its media spend is a decoration on a case study rather than evidence of anything.
